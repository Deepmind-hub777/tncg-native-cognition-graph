# A. Adult Track

## 1. Story opening

Imagine a young woman who loves learning and has become highly skilled in Sanskrit. She notices that many girls and women around her have very limited access to education, especially some child widows whose choices are restricted by social expectations.

She does not only write about the problem. She studies, speaks publicly, builds networks, raises support, and helps establish places where women can learn and live.

Her name is Pandita Ramabai.

Her story is not a simple tale of a Western missionary bringing education to India. Ramabai was an Indian scholar, writer, reformer, Christian, institution-builder, and a person who negotiated with several powerful worlds: Indian social reform movements, Hindu communities, Christian supporters, and the British colonial environment. She did not fit neatly into any one of them.

Today's lesson follows her work while asking how Christian service can honour the dignity, freedom, and agency of the people it serves.

## 2. Three-language story

### 中文

潘迪塔・拉瑪拜（Pandita Ramabai，1858–1922）出生於印度一個重視梵語學習的家庭。她的父親違背當時某些社會習俗，教女兒學習梵語和宗教文獻。她後來以深厚的梵語學識受到學者肯定，獲得「Pandita」這個學者稱號。

她的生命經歷並不平順。她的父母和姊妹在十九世紀七十年代的饑荒中去世；她後來結婚，丈夫於 1882 年去世，她成為帶著年幼女兒的寡婦。她在旅行和公共生活中，逐漸看見不同女性面對的教育、經濟和社會限制。

1883 年，她前往英國，並在英國期間成為基督徒；她也在 Cheltenham Ladies' College 學習。之後，她前往美國，了解教育制度，並向當地支持者介紹她希望在印度建立的教育工作。

1887 年，她以英文出版《The High-Caste Hindu Woman》。這是她自己的著作，也是她向國際讀者提出社會改革訴求的重要文本。閱讀時要記住：這是她從特定歷史位置發出的改革論述，不是對所有印度女性生活的中立、完整統計。

1889 年，她在孟買創辦 Sharada Sadan，為一些童年守寡的女孩和其他女性提供教育與住宿。學校在 1890 年遷往浦那。1896 年中印度發生饑荒後，她的救助工作擴大；1897 年前後，部分工作在 Kedgaon 發展成 Mukti 社群。那裡逐漸結合住宿、教育、職業訓練和照顧工作。

拉瑪拜的工作受到不同群體支持，也引起爭議。她與印度社會改革者、印度教正統人士、殖民政府及海外基督徒支持者之間，既有合作，也有張力。她的基督信仰是她生命的重要部分，但她不是任何外國支持者的被動代理人。

今天，我們可以從她的經歷學習：教育不只是傳授知識，也能增加人的選擇和參與社會的能力。真正尊重人的服事，應當聆聽當事人的需要，保護其自由，並讓受教育者成為自己生命的主體。

### English

Pandita Ramabai (1858–1922) was born into an Indian family that valued Sanskrit learning. Her father taught her Sanskrit and religious literature, challenging social expectations that limited educational opportunities for some girls and women. Scholars later recognized her learning and honoured her with the title Pandita.

Her life included serious hardship. Her parents and sister died during the famines of the 1870s. She later married, but her husband died in 1882, leaving her to care for a young daughter. Through travel and public work, she became increasingly aware of the educational, economic, and social restrictions faced by different groups of women.

In 1883 she travelled to England and became a Christian during her time there. She studied at Cheltenham Ladies' College. She later travelled to the United States, where she learned about educational systems and sought support for educational work in India.

In 1887 she published The High-Caste Hindu Woman in English. This was her own work and an important appeal to international readers for social reform. It should be read as an argument written from a particular historical position, not as a neutral and complete statistical description of every Indian woman's life.

In 1889 she founded Sharada Sadan in Bombay, providing education and accommodation for some child widows and other women. The school moved to Poona in 1890. After famine affected central India in 1896, her relief work expanded. Around 1897, part of the work developed at Kedgaon into the Mukti community, combining residential care, education, vocational learning, and practical support.

Ramabai's work received support from different groups and also generated controversy. She negotiated with Indian social reformers, Hindu communities, the colonial government, and Christian supporters overseas. Her Christian faith was central to her life, but she was not simply an agent carrying out the wishes of foreign supporters.

Her story invites us to see education as more than the transfer of information. Education can increase a person's choices and participation in society. Service that respects human dignity listens to the people concerned, protects their freedom, and recognizes learners as agents in their own lives.

## 3. Historical context and evidence

### 3.1 A short timeline

| Date | Event | Historical note |
|---|---|---|
| 1858 | Ramabai was born | Her family valued Sanskrit learning. |
| 1870s | Family losses during famine | Institutional and biographical accounts describe the deaths of close family members. |
| 1878 | Recognized in Calcutta for Sanskrit learning | The honorific Pandita is associated with her scholarship. |
| 1882 | Her husband died | She was widowed and cared for her young daughter. |
| 1883 | Travelled to England; became Christian during this period | Scholarship places her conversion in 1883; exact details should be treated carefully. |
| 1886–1888 | Travel and study in the United States | She examined educational models and built an international support network. |
| 1887 | Published The High-Caste Hindu Woman | Primary source written by Ramabai herself. |
| 11 March 1889 | Sharada Sadan opened in Bombay | The date is recorded in the Mukti Mission's historical account. |
| 1890 | Sharada Sadan moved to Poona | The institution continued its educational work. |
| 1896–1897 | Famine relief expanded; work developed at Kedgaon | Distinguish the school in Bombay/Poona from the later Kedgaon community. |
| 1919 | Received the Kaiser-i-Hind medal | A recognition from the British colonial government. |
| 1922 | Ramabai died | Her institutional legacy continued after her death. |

### 3.2 What was Sharada Sadan?

Sharada Sadan was a school and residential setting founded in Bombay in 1889. It initially focused especially on child widows and other girls or women who needed educational opportunities.

The Mukti Mission's retrospective account says that Ramabai promised not to pressure pupils to become Christians and that devotional meetings could be attended voluntarily. The same account says the policy of the later Mukti work changed by 1898, when pupils were to receive Christian teaching. These are institutional retrospective descriptions and should not be projected onto every year or every program.

Academic research also shows that Ramabai's religious identity and social work were negotiated amid disagreements with Hindu orthodoxy, Christian evangelicals, donors, and colonial institutions. Her work cannot be reduced either to a purely secular reform project or to a simple foreign missionary enterprise.

### 3.3 Read her own book carefully

The High-Caste Hindu Woman (1887) is a primary source. It gives direct access to Ramabai's own argument and reform concerns.

But a primary source is not automatically neutral. The book was written to persuade readers and support change. It focuses on high-caste Hindu women and should not be treated as a complete description of all women in India, whose experiences varied by region, caste, class, religion, language, and family circumstances.

### 3.4 Historical distinctions

| Claim | Evidence status | How to teach it |
|---|---|---|
| Ramabai lived from 1858 to 1922 | Well established | State directly. |
| She was highly learned in Sanskrit | Well established | Explain the title Pandita as recognition of learning. |
| She became Christian in 1883 | Supported by scholarship | State the year; avoid inventing a conversion scene or dialogue. |
| Sharada Sadan opened in Bombay in 1889 | Supported by institutional history | Distinguish it from later Kedgaon work. |
| The school moved to Poona in 1890 | Supported by institutional history | Include as a separate milestone. |
| Famine relief expanded in 1896–97 | Supported by mission history and scholarship | Avoid presenting all numbers as independently verified. |
| Every Indian woman had the same experience | Not supported | Reject this generalization. |
| Ramabai simply followed foreign missionary instructions | Not supported | Her agency and conflicts with several groups must be recognized. |
| All pupils were forced to convert | Not supported as a blanket statement | Describe the changing institutional policies carefully and cite the source. |

## 4. Biblical and theological reflection

Read:
- Luke 4:18–19 — good news to the poor and release to the oppressed.
- James 2:14–17 — faith expressed through practical care.
- Proverbs 31:8–9 — speak up for those whose voices are not heard.
- Galatians 3:28 — shared standing in Christ.

Questions for reflection:

1. **Dignity:** Do we treat learners as people with gifts and agency, rather than as projects to be managed?
2. **Access:** What prevents a child or woman from participating in education—language, cost, disability, transport, family expectations, or institutional rules?
3. **Freedom:** Can a learner receive educational help without being pressured to attend religious activities or change belief?
4. **Voice:** Are learners and local families involved in decisions about the program?
5. **Faith and action:** Does Christian service become visible through honesty, patience, competence, and care?

Galatians 3:28 speaks about unity and equal standing in Christ. It should not be used as if it automatically proves that every Christian institution has always treated women equally. The verse invites the church to examine whether its practices reflect the dignity it confesses.

## 5. Cross-cultural mission reflection

- Do not describe India as one uniform culture. Region, language, caste, class, religion, and historical period matter.
- Do not assume that a person needs an outsider to explain their own community.
- Do not make education, food, shelter, or health support conditional on prayer, worship attendance, or conversion.
- Explain clearly which activities are educational, which are religious, and which are optional.
- Ask local educators and families what they consider useful and safe.
- Protect children's privacy. Do not publish identifiable stories or photographs without proper consent and safeguarding.
- Measure success by learning, safety, participation, and long-term opportunity—not by religious response.

For a missionary family in Thailand, the practical lesson is to listen to local teachers, parents, and community leaders before designing a program. Translate with local speakers, invite feedback, and allow people to decline without losing access to ordinary services.

## 9. Thai cultural observation

- Thailand includes many regional languages, ethnic communities, religions, and educational settings.
- Do not assume every Thai family has the same expectations about school, gender, or religion.
- Use polite particles appropriately: ครับ is commonly used by male speakers; ค่ะ/คะ by female speakers, with คะ commonly used in questions.
- Ask before helping, photographing, recording, or sharing a learner's story.
- Respect local educators' expertise and the role of families.
- A community program should be transparent about its purpose, costs, data collection, and optional activities.
- Never connect basic assistance with religious participation.

Useful Thai phrases:
- ขออนุญาตก่อนนะครับ/คะ — May I ask permission first?
- คุณสะดวกไหมครับ/คะ? — Is this convenient for you?
- คุณเลือกได้ — You can choose.
- ไม่เป็นไรครับ/ค่ะ — That's okay.
- เราเคารพการตัดสินใจของคุณ — We respect your decision.

With permission from a local school or community group:

- do not require prayer or religious attendance;
- do not record names unless necessary and authorized;
- ask for feedback;
- let participants decline without explanation.

# B. Children's Track

## 12. Story opening

Long ago in India, there was a girl named Ramabai who loved learning.

Her family taught her Sanskrit. Later, people recognized how much she had learned and called her Pandita, a title for a learned woman.

As an adult, Ramabai noticed that some girls and women had very few chances to study. She wanted them to have places where they could learn, read, write, and develop useful skills.

She helped start a school called Sharada Sadan in 1889.

Her story reminds us that learning can open doors. It also reminds us to ask what people want to learn and to respect their choices.

### English

Ramabai was an Indian woman who loved learning.

Her family taught her Sanskrit. Later, scholars admired her knowledge and called her Pandita, a title for a learned woman.

As she grew older, she noticed that some girls and women had few chances to go to school.

She wanted more people to have opportunities to learn. In 1889, she started a school called Sharada Sadan in Bombay, where some girls and women could study and have a place to stay.

Later, her work grew to support more women and children in need.

We can learn from her example: study carefully, care about others, and respect everyone's choices.

## 24. Evidence labels and graph quality rules

Use these labels:
- **Primary source:** Ramabai's own 1887 book.
- **Institutional retrospective:** Mukti Mission's history pages.
- **Academic interpretation:** peer-reviewed or scholarly studies.
- **Teaching application:** modern ethical reflection, not a historical claim.

Quality rules:
1. Do not treat one book as a complete description of all Indian women.
2. Do not collapse all Hindu, Christian, or Indian communities into one group.
3. Keep Sharada Sadan's Bombay/Poona history distinct from later Kedgaon developments.
4. Record the changing policy on religious instruction with dates and source attribution.
5. Do not invent conversations or quotations.
6. Preserve the agency of women and learners in graph relationships.
7. Mark uncertain numbers as institutional reports rather than independently verified totals.
8. Separate biblical interpretation from historical evidence.
